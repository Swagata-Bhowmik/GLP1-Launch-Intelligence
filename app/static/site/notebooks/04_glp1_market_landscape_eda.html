💡 Concept · EDA as a sequence of questions

EDA (exploratory data analysis) isn't "make lots of charts". It's asking questions and letting the data answer:

  1. How big is the market and who leads? → share by brand
  2. Where is Lilly's tirzepatide strong or weak? → share by state and specialty
  3. What is changing right now? → Medicaid quarterly trends to 2026
  4. Why did something change? → test hypotheses against real-world events
  5. What are doctors hearing about? → side-effect reports and research momentum

Choosing a chart: the data's job picks the form. Comparing sizes → bars; change over time → lines; a single headline → a number, not a chart. Colour follows the molecule in every chart: ■ tirzepatide · ■ semaglutide · ■ dulaglutide · ■ liraglutide. Obesity brands reuse their molecule's colour with dashed lines / hatching, so identity never relies on colour alone (colour-blind safe).

2 · Where is tirzepatide strong or weak?¶

💡 Concept

"Share" only means something against a fair comparison. We use tirzepatide ÷ (tirzepatide + semaglutide): the head-to-head between the two leading molecules. A diverging layout around the national average shows which states are above (stronger) or below (weaker).

In [3]:
states = duckdb.sql(f"""
    SELECT  Prscrbr_State_Abrvtn AS state,
            SUM(Tot_Clms) FILTER (WHERE Generic = 'Tirzepatide')                                  AS tirz,
            SUM(Tot_Clms) FILTER (WHERE Generic IN ('Tirzepatide', 'Semaglutide'))               AS head_to_head
    FROM    {GLP1}
    GROUP BY ALL
    HAVING  head_to_head > 50000                                        -- skip territories with tiny volume
""").df()
states["share"] = 100 * states["tirz"] / states["head_to_head"]
national = 100 * states["tirz"].sum() / states["head_to_head"].sum()
top_bottom = pd.concat([states.nsmallest(10, "share"), states.nlargest(10, "share")]).sort_values("share")

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8.5, 6))
diff = top_bottom["share"] - national
ax.barh(top_bottom["state"], diff, left=national, height=0.62,
        color=[MOLECULE_COLOR["tirzepatide"] if d > 0 else MOLECULE_COLOR["semaglutide"] for d in diff])
ax.axvline(national, color=TEXT_2, lw=1)
ax.text(national, len(top_bottom) - 0.3, f" US average {national:.0f}%", color=TEXT_2, fontsize=9, va="bottom")
for y, (s, v) in enumerate(zip(top_bottom["state"], top_bottom["share"])):
    ax.text(v + (0.6 if v > national else -0.6), y, f"{v:.0f}%", va="center",
            ha="left" if v > national else "right", fontsize=9, color=TEXT)
ax.set_title("Tirzepatide share swings from ~18% to ~45% by state")
ax.set_xlim(top_bottom["share"].min() - 4, top_bottom["share"].max() + 3)
subtitle(ax, "Tirzepatide ÷ (tirzepatide + semaglutide), Part D 2024 · 10 lowest and 10 highest states")
ax.xaxis.set_major_formatter(mtick.PercentFormatter(decimals=0))
viz.save(fig, "04_2_tirzepatide_share_by_state")
plt.show()

📊 Reading the output

  • Same drug, same year, so the difference comes from local factors: payer formularies, sales-force coverage, specialist density, prescriber habits. For a sales-optimisation team this map is the starting question: "Why are we weaker in California, and is it fixable?"

4 · Why did obesity prescriptions drop in Q1 2026? Testing two hypotheses¶

💡 Concept · Hypothesis-driven analysis

Never explain a surprise with the first story that comes to mind. Write down the competing hypotheses and test each against the data:

Hypothesis If true, we'd see…
H1 · Late reporting: Q1 2026 is incomplete fewer states reporting, and all brands down
H2 · Policy change: some states stopped covering obesity GLP-1s normal reporting, diabetes brands flat, drop concentrated in a few states

# ── H2: where did the obesity-brand drop happen? ────────────────────────────
ob = (sdud[sdud["brand"].isin(["Zepbound", "Wegovy"])]
      .pivot_table(index="state", columns="period", values="rx", aggfunc="sum").fillna(0))
chg = pd.DataFrame({"q4_2025": ob["2025 Q4"], "q1_2026": ob["2026 Q1"]})
chg["change"] = chg["q1_2026"] - chg["q4_2025"]
chg["pct"] = 100 * chg["change"] / chg["q4_2025"]
big = chg[chg["q4_2025"] > 3000].sort_values("pct")                      # states with meaningful volume

POLICY = {"CA", "PA", "NH", "SC"}                                         # ended obesity coverage 1 Jan 2026
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8.5, 6.5))
CAP = 100                                                               # cap the axis so one outlier doesn't squash the rest
ax.barh(big.index, big["pct"].clip(upper=CAP), height=0.62,
        color=["#FF8A65" if s in POLICY else MUTED for s in big.index])
for y, (s, p) in enumerate(zip(big.index, big["pct"])):
    shown = min(p, CAP)
    label = f"{p:+.0f}% →" if p > CAP else f"{p:+.0f}%"
    ax.text(shown + (-2 if p < 0 else 2), y, label, va="center", ha="right" if p < 0 else "left", fontsize=8.5, color=TEXT)
ax.axvline(0, color=TEXT_2, lw=1)
ax.set_title("The drop is concentrated in states that ended coverage")
subtitle(ax, "Zepbound + Wegovy Medicaid Rx, change Q4 2025 → Q1 2026 · orange = state ended obesity coverage 1 Jan 2026")
ax.xaxis.set_major_formatter(mtick.PercentFormatter(decimals=0))
ax.set_xlim(-135, CAP + 25)
viz.save(fig, "04_5_medicaid_coverage_cuts")
plt.show()

share_of_drop = chg.loc[chg.index.isin(POLICY), "change"].sum() / chg["change"].sum()
policy_drop = chg.loc[chg.index.isin(POLICY), "change"].sum()
print(f"Drop in the 4 states that ended coverage : {policy_drop:,.0f} prescriptions")
print(f"Net national change (all states)        : {chg['change'].sum():,.0f} prescriptions")

📊 Reading the output

  • H1 rejected: all 52 states/territories reported in every quarter, and diabetes brands didn't fall.
  • H2 supported: California (−76%), Pennsylvania (−96%), New Hampshire (−88%) and South Carolina (−62%) lost more prescriptions than the whole national net drop; many other states kept growing.
  • Two oddities worth a footnote: Puerto Rico reports zero (likely a reporting gap, not policy) and North Carolina jumps +387% (its obesity coverage changed during 2025; the bar is capped at +100%).
  • External check: California's Medi-Cal, Pennsylvania's Medicaid, New Hampshire and South Carolina all stopped covering GLP-1s for weight loss on 1 January 2026, keeping diabetes coverage (sources: CMA, PHLP, Philadelphia Inquirer / KFF, April 2026).

🎯 The headline insight

Coverage decides volume. When Medicaid covered obesity GLP-1s, prescriptions rose 27×; when four states removed coverage, volume collapsed within one quarter. The Medicare GLP-1 Bridge (July 2026) is the same lever pulled the other way: millions of 65+ patients gain $50/month access. That's why the targeting model (notebook 05) weights coverage and patient base, not just past prescribing.

⚠️ Watch out

Medicaid ≠ Medicare. Medicaid patients are younger and lower-income; the Bridge affects older patients. The direction (coverage → volume) transfers; the size won't be identical.

⚠️ Watch out

Never present FAERS counts as side-effect rates: there's no denominator (number of patients exposed), reporting is voluntary, and media attention and lawsuits stimulate reports. In the product, FAERS is used to know which concerns doctors are likely to raise (nausea, dosing errors), not to compare drug safety.

6 · The market story in five sentences¶

🎯 Key takeaways

  1. Semaglutide leads Medicare 2024 (Ozempic 9.9M claims), with tirzepatide (Mounjaro 4.7M) a fast-growing #2; Novo ~56% vs Lilly ~43%.
  2. Tirzepatide share varies 2.5× by state (≈18% in Oregon, 19% in California vs ≈45% in Utah) and is highest with endocrinologists: local factors matter.
  3. Coverage decides volume: Medicaid obesity GLP-1s rose 27× in two years, then fell 40–50% in one quarter when four states ended coverage (Jan 2026).
  4. The Medicare Bridge (Jul 2026) reverses that lever for 65+ patients, so prescribers already treating older diabetes and obesity patients are the first opportunity.
  5. Doctors face a flood of information (safety reports, thousands of papers), the case for AI-generated, evidence-cited briefs.

Check your understanding

  • How do you pick a chart type? By the data's job: magnitude → bars, change → lines, one headline → a number.
  • How did you explain the Q1 2026 drop? Two competing hypotheses, tested in the data (reporting completeness, geographic concentration), then confirmed with external sources.
  • Why can't FAERS rank drug safety? No denominator, voluntary, stimulated reporting.
  • Why colour by molecule? Colour follows the entity, so the same colour means the same thing in every chart; hatching/dashes keep it colour-blind safe.
